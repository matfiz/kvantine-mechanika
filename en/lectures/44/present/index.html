Lecture 44

Collapse and branching: GRW and Everett

GRW changes universal unitary dynamics; Everett changes the requirement of one global outcome. We compute the exact and approximate claims behind a GRW localisation, and prove that relative branches reproduce Lecture 40’s full reading-chain probabilities and conditional states. A common comparison then returns to the course’s question: what data specify a quantum system, and which physical commitments remain open?

The physics

The parameters are conventional hypotheses, not measured constants. Bassi et al., RMP 85, 471 (2013), review model-dependent experimental constraints as of that review. GRW predicts extra coherence loss and momentum noise even when known environmental noise is removed; its non-dissipative kicks also inject mean kinetic energy. Calling that a temperature rise needs an equilibration and heat-capacity model. Environmental scattering can exchange energy too. Isolated-system residual noise and matter-wave visibility, rather than the word “heating” alone, provide tests. Lecture 41’s air-grain estimate concerns a different object and reservoir, not a numerical measurement of this pointer’s collapse rate. The labelled calculation is a simplifying model: individual labelled hits need not preserve an identical-fermion subspace. Symmetry-preserving collapse models require further specification. GRWm’s matter density and GRWf’s flashes are distinct choices of physical ontology for records.

44.4 Weights, probabilities and the same comparison questions

The identity uses squared norms and the instrument probabilities already defined by Born’s rule. It does not derive the interpretation of those weights as probabilities from unitary dynamics alone, or select one global branch. Wallace’s decision-theoretic programme is a named representation argument with additional assumptions: sufficiently rich available acts, coherent preferences across decisions, continuity, preferences depending only on the resulting state (state supervenience), macrostate indifference, and indifference to branch splitting that leaves rewards unchanged (§5.6 pp. 174–181; theorem discussion §5.7 p. 182 onward). Its conclusion is reported under those axioms, not as a proof from U alone. Here rich available acts mean that the compared decisions can actually be implemented within the theorem’s class of transformations. Coherent preferences require consistent choices when the same decision is divided into stages. Continuity controls what happens under small changes of an act. State supervenience says that two acts with the same resulting state have the same preference. Macrostate indifference removes a preference for microscopic differences within one rewarded macrostate. Branch-splitting indifference removes a preference for extra branches when their rewards are unchanged. These are decision assumptions in addition to unitary dynamics, rather than consequences of the chain identity. Counting branches is not supplied as an alternative probability law.

Locality also needs the model specified. GRW does not inherit PI merely from calling its wavefunction complete. For separate noninteracting labs, averaging a remote hit gives a local TP map; its centre probability cancels the conditional normalisation, leaving the other lab’s marginal unchanged (cited: Tumulka, §5.5.9 pp. 232–234). At the predictive wavefunction-state level this gives PI under the stated setting assumptions; Bell-violating correlations then require OI to fail. Conditioning on future realised collapse centres would define a different fine description. The selected wavefunction changes nonlocally even when averaged marginals do not signal. Everett likewise retains the Bell correlations; rejecting a unique global outcome is not construction of a classical factorising single-outcome model (Wallace, §8.7 p. 310).

The physics

Other approaches make different commitments. Copenhagen accounts specify a classical measurement context for applying the quantum rules. QBism interprets state probabilities as an agent’s commitments, with Born’s rule a consistency constraint, rather than a microscopic collapse mechanism (Fuchs–Schack, RMP 85, 1693 (2013)). Consistent histories assigns classical joint probabilities only within an appropriate consistent history family; its single-framework rule forbids freely combining incompatible families (Griffiths, Ch. 16 pp. 216–219; the histories construction is in Ch. 10 pp. 137–147). These are stated positions, not conclusions selected by our no-go proof.

44.5 The quantum-system specification

Definition 44.3 (The course’s system card). A quantum-system specification gives a nonzero separable Hilbert space, its admitted observable algebra and normal states, allowed dynamics and symmetries, the composite rule, and the instruments used for observations.

For the full algebra B(H)\mathcal B(\Hil)B(H), states are exactly Lecture 31’s density operators and pure states are Lecture 32’s rays. With the two-sector algebra of Lecture 35, states instead have the unique block-diagonal representatives proved there; its pure states are single-sector rays. Unbounded observables must have spectral projections in the admitted algebra. State and purity are relative to that algebra, not to a choice of Hilbert-space representative alone.

Allowed symmetries must respect this specification. Within a coherent sector Wigner supplies unitary or antiunitary ray implementations. Sector-preserving ones act within sectors; more general algebra-preserving discrete symmetries may permute equivalent sectors. For example, swapping two isomorphic blocks sends A1⊕A2A_1\oplus A_2A1​⊕A2​ to A2⊕A1A_2\oplus A_1A2​⊕A1​ and preserves the block algebra without fixing its sector projections. Which such maps are physical symmetries is additional data. The baseline closed evolution is a sector-compatible unitary group under Lecture 35’s continuity and lift premises; GRW deliberately changes that dynamical law.

Composites use Lecture 36’s Hilbert tensor product. Their observable algebra must also be specified: imposing each part’s sector restriction and imposing only a total conserved charge are different choices. For systems without superselection the course used the full composite algebra. A part initially prepared independently of its environment has the channel description proved in Lecture 37; the finite semigroup model has Lecture 38’s GKSL structure.

For a worked Lecture 35-type card, take H=C⊕C2\Hil=\C\oplus\C^2H=C⊕C2, A=C⊕M2\mathcal A=\C\oplus M_2A=C⊕M2​, ρ=1/3⊕(2/3)∣+x⟩ ⁣⟨+x∣\rho=1/3\oplus(2/3)\proj{+x}ρ=1/3⊕(2/3)∣+x⟩⟨+x∣ and H=0⊕(ℏν/2)σzH=0\oplus(\hbar\nu/2)\sigma_zH=0⊕(ℏν/2)σz​. Time evolution is the corresponding block unitary. The sector instrument Ij(ρ)=EjρEj\mathcal I_j(\rho)=E_j\rho E_jIj​(ρ)=Ej​ρEj​ has effects Ej∈AE_j\in\mathcal AEj​∈A, probabilities 1/3,2/31/3,2/31/3,2/3, and admissible conditional states 111 and ∣+x⟩ ⁣⟨+x∣\proj{+x}∣+x⟩⟨+x∣ at this initial time. With an ordinary qubit partner, choose composite algebra M2⊕M4M_2\oplus M_4M2​⊕M4​ on the two composite sectors. This specifies the algebra as well as the tensor space.

Effects alone do not ensure that an operation descends to admitted states. On the simpler algebra C⊕C\C\oplus\CC⊕C, the ray ∣+x⟩ ⁣⟨+x∣\proj{+x}∣+x⟩⟨+x∣ and 112/2\Id_2/2112​/2 represent the same restricted state. The one-outcome channel with K0=∣0⟩⟨+x∣K_0=\ket0\bra{+x}K0​=∣0⟩⟨+x∣, K1=∣1⟩⟨−x∣K_1=\ket1\bra{-x}K1​=∣1⟩⟨−x∣ has total effect 11\Id11 in that algebra, yet sends those representatives to ∣0⟩ ⁣⟨0∣\proj0∣0⟩⟨0∣ and 112/2\Id_2/2112​/2. Their later sector probabilities differ. Hence an instrument must act unambiguously on the admitted states, with admissible outputs, not merely have effects in the algebra. Operations defined directly on block-diagonal states avoid that representative dependence; permitted sector transfers are additional physical data, not a requirement that each Kraus operator commute with the sector sign.

This common operational structure leaves Lecture 42’s C/U/D question open. Bohm adds configurations; GRW changes dynamics and specifies record ontology; Everett changes the account of global versus relative records. The card states what each proposal must specify and what its predictions can be compared against, without declaring one interpretation a theorem.

What is a quantum system, and what remains open?

The observable algebra and its normal states specify expectations and events; tensor products specify composites, compatible dynamics specify change, and instruments specify observations. On the full algebra the states are density operators; superselection changes their identification and purity. The formalism predicts complete reading-chain statistics, but universal unitarity, completeness and one global outcome cannot all hold in Lecture 42’s calibrated model; which premise to change is a further physical or interpretive choice. The three responses were compared by the same mathematical and experimental questions.

Summary

GRW’s expected branch weights are exact, whereas one-hit selection needs separation and a tail estimate. Its Poisson jump kernel gives labelled amplification, with explicit modelling limits. Everett’s finite branch weights and reduced relative states reproduce the full capstone, including a mixed input’s reference trace. That identity leaves the probability interpretation as a further argument. The system card keeps states, algebras, dynamics and instruments together and makes the remaining commitments explicit.
