Lecture 31

What a state is

A state assigns expectations to observables. In a separable Hilbert space, positivity, normalisation and countable additivity on orthogonal events make this rule a density operator. This includes infinitely many populated levels, but excludes an exactly sharp position.

The inner product ⟨φ,ψ⟩\inner\varphi\psi⟨φ,ψ⟩ is antilinear in its first argument and linear in its second. The space H\HilH is a Hilbert space, whereas HHH denotes a Hamiltonian. The expectation rule is ω\omegaω; angular frequency is ν\nuν. We use EEE for an event projection and reserve PPP for momentum. Tensor-factor order will be stated when a composite system is introduced.

Facts of analysis used in Part III. We follow the proof conventions of §15.3 and recall its standing facts S1–S4. We accept S5 and S6 below without proof.

31.1 A rule for expectations

In Part I, a preparation chose a unit vector ψk\psi_kψk​ with probability pkp_kpk​. Every bounded observable AAA then had mean ∑kpk⟨ψk,Aψk⟩\sum_k p_k\inner{\psi_k}{A\psi_k}∑k​pk​⟨ψk​,Aψk​⟩. The density matrix collected those means into Tr⁡(ρA)\Tr(\rho A)Tr(ρA) (Definition 3.3 and Theorem 3.4); different preparations could give the same matrix. Part II described a particle on the line by rays in L2(R)L^2(\R)L2(R) (§30.4). Does a density operator still give the means when the space has infinitely many dimensions?

Start with the means themselves. We work on a nonzero separable complex Hilbert space H\HilH: it has a finite or countable orthonormal basis (ONB). Write B(H)B(\Hil)B(H) for its bounded operators. A bounded observable is a self-adjoint member of this space. An event is an orthogonal projection EEE; its expectation is its probability. Even for an unbounded observable, such as position QQQ, its spectral projections EQ(I)E_Q(I)EQ​(I) belong to B(H)B(\Hil)B(H). Knowing their probabilities determines the distribution of its readings. Its mean may still be infinite or undefined.

It is convenient to assign a complex number to every bounded operator: write A=C+iDA=C+\ii DA=C+iD with C=(A+A†)/2C=(A+\adj A)/2C=(A+A†)/2, D=(A−A†)/(2i)D=(A-\adj A)/(2\ii)D=(A−A†)/(2i) self-adjoint, and extend the expectation by complex linearity.

Definition 31.1 (An expectation rule). An algebraic state is a complex-linear map ω:B(H)→C\omega:B(\Hil)\to\Cω:B(H)→C such that ω(B)≥0\omega(B)\geq0ω(B)≥0 for every positive operator BBB, and ω(11)=1\omega(\Id)=1ω(11)=1. The letter ω\omegaω denotes an expectation rule, never a frequency; our angular frequency is ν\nuν. Positivity means that a nonnegative observable has a nonnegative mean. Linearity is a requirement on expectations, including those of noncommuting observables; it does not assert that they have simultaneous values. We will add one continuity requirement before calling such a rule a state.

31.2 Positive trace class

The finite-dimensional trace is a sum of diagonal entries. In infinite dimension a positive operator’s sum may diverge: the identity has one on every diagonal entry. We first build the part of trace theory needed for states. The two standing facts used here are S5, the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators, and S6, existence of a positive square root. They are stated in the standing-fact list at the start of this lecture.

Definition 31.3 (Positive trace class and density operators). A positive bounded operator ρ\rhoρ is positive trace class if, for one ONB (ek)(e_k)(ek​), ∑k⟨ek,ρek⟩<∞\sum_k\inner{e_k}{\rho e_k}<\infty∑k​⟨ek​,ρek​⟩<∞. This sum is its trace, Tr⁡ρ\Tr\rhoTrρ; for a divergent positive sum write Tr⁡ρ=+∞\Tr\rho=+\inftyTrρ=+∞. A density operator is a positive trace-class operator with Tr⁡ρ=1\Tr\rho=1Trρ=1.

The words “for one ONB” are enough: the next proposition proves that the sum is independent of the basis. A positive operator of infinite trace is not trace class. The infinite value is basis independent too: a finite sum in another basis would, by the proposition, make every basis sum finite.

Proposition 31.4 (The trace and its spectral form). Every positive trace-class operator is compact. It has an orthonormal eigenbasis (φj)(\varphi_j)(φj​) with eigenvalues pj≥0p_j\geq0pj​≥0, and ρ=∑jpj∣φj⟩ ⁣⟨φj∣,Tr⁡ρ=∑jpj=∑k⟨ek,ρek⟩\begin{equation} \label{eq:31:trace} \rho=\sum_j p_j\proj{\varphi_j},\qquad \Tr\rho=\sum_jp_j=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{\rho e_k} \end{equation}ρ=j∑​pj​∣φj​⟩⟨φj​∣,Trρ=j∑​pj​=k∑​⟨ek​,ρek​⟩(31.2) for every ONB (ek)(e_k)(ek​). For a density operator, this decomposition supplies one possible preparation; it does not single out how the state was prepared. Lecture 32 studies that freedom. The proof first turns a small diagonal tail into an operator-norm approximation by finite-rank operators. Only after this compactness step may we use S5 to choose an eigenbasis. Parseval’s identity then compares the trace sums in different bases. This order avoids assuming that a general bounded positive operator already has an eigenbasis.

31.3 Normal states are density operators

If (Ek)(E_k)(Ek​) are mutually orthogonal event projections, their sum is an operator defined by its action: ∑kEkψ=lim⁡N→∞∑k≤NEkψ\sum_k E_k\psi=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k\leq N}E_k\psi∑k​Ek​ψ=limN→∞​∑k≤N​Ek​ψ for each ψ\psiψ. Convergence need not hold in operator norm: for basis truncations ENE_NEN​, ENψ→ψE_N\psi\to\psiEN​ψ→ψ but ∥11−EN∥=1\norm{\Id-E_N}=1∥11−EN​∥=1 in infinite dimension. The bound in Lemma 31.2 alone cannot pass to such a limit.

Definition 31.5 (Normality). An algebraic state is normal if every countable orthogonal family of projections satisfies ω(∑kEk)=∑kω(Ek).\begin{equation} \label{eq:31:normal} \omega\Bigl(\sum_k E_k\Bigr)=\sum_k\omega(E_k). \end{equation}ω(k∑​Ek​)=k∑​ω(Ek​).(31.5) In Part III, state means a normal algebraic state.

Normality is countable additivity of probabilities for exclusive events. In particular, if an ONB exhausts the possible alternatives, their probabilities must sum to 1. It rules out assigning probability zero to every one of those alternatives while assigning probability one to their union. It is an assumption about the expectation rule, not about any particular preparation.

Uniqueness. For any density operator σ\sigmaσ, Equation (31.4) gives Tr⁡(σ∣ψ⟩ ⁣⟨φ∣)=⟨φ,σψ⟩\Tr(\sigma\ketbra\psi\varphi)=\inner\varphi{\sigma\psi}Tr(σ∣ψ⟩⟨φ∣)=⟨φ,σψ⟩. If two density operators give the same rule, all their matrix elements agree, so they are equal.

The theorem recovers the density matrices of Part I from rules for expectations, without first choosing an ensemble. This is not peculiar to position space: every separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is unitarily equivalent to L2(R)L^2(\R)L2(R), by sending one ONB to another. The counterexample below therefore transfers to any such space.

A trapped ion’s motion in one approximately harmonic direction is an oscillator. Its motional temperature can be expressed as a mean level number. What is a state? First answer

A state is a positive, normalised linear rule for bounded expectations, countably additive on orthogonal events. On B(H)B(\Hil)B(H) for separable H\HilH, it is exactly ω(A)=Tr⁡(ρA)\omega(A)=\Tr(\rho A)ω(A)=Tr(ρA) for one density operator. A sharp event EA(I)E_A(I)EA​(I) has probability Tr⁡(ρEA(I))\Tr(\rho E_A(I))Tr(ρEA​(I)). Unbounded means require an additional convergence statement. Neither a preparation ensemble nor a measurement update is specified by this definition.

Summary

Finite positive trace forces compactness, and the trace is basis independent. Positivity and Cauchy–Schwarz reconstruct an operator from rank-one expectations; normality makes its trace one and recovers all bounded expectations. The thermal oscillator is an infinite-rank example. An exactly sharp position cannot be represented by any density operator. Next we ask how many preparations one state can have.
